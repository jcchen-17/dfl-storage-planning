\section{Carbon-Constrained Shared-Storage Planning}
\label{sec:model}

% ---------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Carbon Target and Its Economic Treatment}
\label{sec:carbon_target}

The facility is held to a carbon-intensity target
$\overline{\mathscr{w}}$ on delivered power. Writing the system carbon
flow and the system power at interval $(s,d,t)$ as
\begin{align}
\Phi_{s,d,t}
&=
\mathscr{w}_{s,d,t}^{\mathrm{grid}}P_{s,d,t}^{\mathrm{grid}}
+\sum_{i\in\mathcal{N}}\sum_{g\in\mathcal{G}_i}
\mathscr{w}_{g}^{G}P_{g,s,d,t}
+\sum_{i\in\mathcal{N}^{\mathrm{es}}}
R_{i,s,d,t}^{\mathrm{es,dc}},
\label{eq:system_carbon}\\
\Psi_{s,d,t}
&=
P_{s,d,t}^{\mathrm{grid}}
+\sum_{i\in\mathcal{N}}\sum_{g\in\mathcal{G}_i}P_{g,s,d,t}
+\sum_{i\in\mathcal{N}}P_{i,s,d,t}^{\mathrm{PV}}
+\sum_{i\in\mathcal{N}^{\mathrm{es}}}P_{i,s,d,t}^{\mathrm{es,dc}},
\label{eq:system_power}
\end{align}
the target requires
$\Phi_{s,d,t}\leq\overline{\mathscr{w}}\Psi_{s,d,t}$, with
$R_{i,s,d,t}^{\mathrm{es,dc}}$ given by \eqref{eq:storage_carbon_flow}
so that the vintage structure of the stored energy enters the target
directly.

Imposing this as a hard constraint is unsatisfactory for planning. A
target tight enough to influence the storage decision is, on this system,
already tight enough to be unreachable in some intervals, and the
planning problem then returns no design at all rather than an expensive
one. We therefore admit an excess $\Xi_{s,d,t}\geq0$, measured in the
same units as $\Phi_{s,d,t}$, and price it:
\begin{equation}
\Phi_{s,d,t}
\leq
\overline{\mathscr{w}}\Psi_{s,d,t}
+
\Xi_{s,d,t}.
\label{eq:carbon_target}
\end{equation}
The operating cost \eqref{eq:interval_cost} gains the term
$c^{\mathrm{CO_2}}\Xi_{s,d,t}$, where $c^{\mathrm{CO_2}}$ is a price per
unit of carbon emitted beyond the target.

This differs from the usual big-$M$ relaxation, in which a dimensionless
violation variable is scaled by a bound on system power before entering
the constraint. Under that formulation the implied price per unit of
carbon depends on the numerical bound rather than on any modelling
assumption, so the value attributed to storage moves when the bound is
re-derived. Pricing $\Xi_{s,d,t}$ in physical units removes that
dependence: $c^{\mathrm{CO_2}}$ is an assumption of the study, stated
once, and the storage value it produces varies smoothly with the target
$\overline{\mathscr{w}}$ rather than jumping when the target crosses
feasibility.

The formulation admits both limiting cases. Letting
$c^{\mathrm{CO_2}}\!\to\!\infty$ recovers the hard cap wherever it is
attainable, and $c^{\mathrm{CO_2}}\!=\!0$ recovers the carbon-agnostic
planning problem, so the target's influence on siting and sizing can be
traced continuously between them.

% ---------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Where the Storage Value Originates}
\label{sec:value_origin}

Combining \eqref{eq:carbon_target} with \eqref{eq:storage_carbon_flow}
isolates what storage contributes to compliance. Collecting the terms
that do not involve storage discharge into
\begin{equation}
D_{s,d,t}
=
\Phi_{s,d,t}^{\setminus\mathrm{es}}
-
\overline{\mathscr{w}}\,\Psi_{s,d,t}^{\setminus\mathrm{es}},
\label{eq:carbon_gap_system}
\end{equation}
the target becomes
\begin{equation}
\sum_{i\in\mathcal{N}^{\mathrm{es}}}\sum_{k\in\mathcal{K}}
\bigl(\overline{\mathscr{w}}-\mathscr{w}_{i,k,s}^{\mathrm{es}}\bigr)
P_{i,k,s,d,t}^{\mathrm{es,dc}}
+
\Xi_{s,d,t}
\geq
D_{s,d,t}.
\label{eq:carbon_gap_priced}
\end{equation}

Equation \eqref{eq:carbon_gap_priced} states the mechanism the model is
built to represent. A positive $D_{s,d,t}$ is a compliance deficit that
must be met either by discharging stored energy whose vintage intensity
is below the target, at a rate weighted by how far below it lies, or by
paying for the excess. The planner therefore has an incentive to charge
during low-intensity intervals specifically so that the resulting
vintages can be discharged when $D_{s,d,t}$ is positive, and the value of
a given installation depends on the intensities of the vintages it can
hold rather than on its energy capacity alone. A well-mixed inventory
model cannot express this, since it assigns a single intensity to all
stored energy and so cannot distinguish a battery charged at the cleanest
hours from one charged at the dirtiest.

\section{Decision-Focused Scenario Selection}
\label{sec:dfl}

% ---------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{The Selection Problem}
\label{sec:selection_problem}

Let $\xi$ denote an operating scenario---a joint realization of load,
PV availability, workload arrivals, grid carbon intensity and prices over
the planning horizon---distributed as $\mathbb{P}$. Writing
$c(\boldsymbol{x}^{\mathrm{pl}},\xi)$ for the optimal operating cost of
the second stage under design $\boldsymbol{x}^{\mathrm{pl}}$ and scenario
$\xi$, the planning problem of interest is
\begin{equation}
\min_{\boldsymbol{x}^{\mathrm{pl}}}
\quad
C^{\mathrm{inv}}(\boldsymbol{x}^{\mathrm{pl}})
+
\mathbb{E}_{\xi\sim\mathbb{P}}
\bigl[c(\boldsymbol{x}^{\mathrm{pl}},\xi)\bigr].
\label{eq:true_problem}
\end{equation}
Each evaluation of $c$ requires solving a mixed-integer program over the
full horizon, so \eqref{eq:true_problem} is solved in practice on a
support of $K$ scenarios $\mathcal{X}=\{\xi_1,\dots,\xi_K\}$ with weights
$\boldsymbol{\pi}\in\Delta^{K-1}$:
\begin{equation}
\boldsymbol{x}^{\star}(\mathcal{X},\boldsymbol{\pi})
\in
\argmin_{\boldsymbol{x}^{\mathrm{pl}}}
\;
C^{\mathrm{inv}}(\boldsymbol{x}^{\mathrm{pl}})
+
\sum_{k=1}^{K}\pi_k\,c(\boldsymbol{x}^{\mathrm{pl}},\xi_k).
\label{eq:support_problem}
\end{equation}

The support is conventionally chosen to represent $\mathbb{P}$ in
distribution, by clustering or by maximizing coverage. That criterion is
indifferent to what the support is for. Two supports that are equally
representative in a distributional sense can induce designs of very
different quality, because \eqref{eq:support_problem} is sensitive only
to the features of $\xi$ that bind its constraints. We therefore select
the support by the quality of the decision it induces:
\begin{equation}
\min_{\mathcal{X},\boldsymbol{\pi}}
\quad
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{X},\boldsymbol{\pi})
:=
\mathbb{E}_{\xi\sim\mathbb{P}}
\bigl[
c\bigl(\boldsymbol{x}^{\star}(\mathcal{X},\boldsymbol{\pi}),\xi\bigr)
\bigr].
\label{eq:decision_loss}
\end{equation}
The support enters \eqref{eq:decision_loss} only through the design it
produces; a scenario that is unusual but does not change
$\boldsymbol{x}^{\star}$ has no effect on the loss.

% ---------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Generative Parameterization}
\label{sec:parameterization}

Searching over $\mathcal{X}$ directly is unattractive: the scenarios are
high-dimensional and must remain physically admissible, and restricting
the search to observed scenarios reduces \eqref{eq:decision_loss} to a
combinatorial subset selection over the historical record.

We instead search in the latent space of a conditional generator
$G_{\phi}$ trained beforehand on the historical pool, which maps a latent
$\boldsymbol{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_z}$ and a context
$\boldsymbol{\omega}$ to a scenario. Writing
$\boldsymbol{Z}=[\boldsymbol{z}_1,\dots,\boldsymbol{z}_K]$ and
$\boldsymbol{\Omega}=[\boldsymbol{\omega}_1,\dots,\boldsymbol{\omega}_K]$,
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{X}(\boldsymbol{Z},\boldsymbol{\Omega})
=
\bigl\{
G_{\phi}(\boldsymbol{z}_k,\boldsymbol{\omega}_k)
\bigr\}_{k=1}^{K},
\label{eq:generated_support}
\end{equation}
and the weights are parameterized by logits $\boldsymbol{\lambda}$ through
$\boldsymbol{\pi}=\mathrm{softmax}(\boldsymbol{\lambda})$. The generator
is held fixed; only $(\boldsymbol{Z},\boldsymbol{\lambda})$ are optimized.
The support is thus free to leave the historical record while remaining on
the manifold the generator learned, and the search is over $Kn_z$
continuous variables rather than over subsets.

The contexts $\boldsymbol{\Omega}$ are set from $K$ observed scenarios
chosen by a coverage rule and then held fixed, which anchors each support
member to a distinct region of the conditioning space. This is a
restriction: any attribute of $\xi$ carried by the context rather than by
the latent cannot be altered by the search, and the reachable set is
correspondingly a neighbourhood of the anchors rather than the generator's
full range.

% ---------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Score-Function Optimization}
\label{sec:score_function}

The composition in \eqref{eq:decision_loss} is not differentiable in
$(\boldsymbol{Z},\boldsymbol{\lambda})$. The map from support to design is
the $\argmin$ of a mixed-integer program, so it is piecewise constant:
almost everywhere its gradient vanishes, and where the active set changes
it does not exist. Neither differentiating through the solver nor
smoothing its optimality conditions applies once integer siting variables
are present.

We therefore treat the support as a random variable and optimize the
expected loss. Let
\begin{equation}
q_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{Z})
=
\mathcal{N}\bigl(\boldsymbol{Z};\boldsymbol{\mu},\sigma^2\boldsymbol{I}\bigr),
\qquad
\boldsymbol{\theta}=(\boldsymbol{\mu},\boldsymbol{\lambda}),
\label{eq:policy}
\end{equation}
and minimize
$\mathcal{J}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=
\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{Z}\sim q_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}
[\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{X}(\boldsymbol{Z},\boldsymbol{\Omega}),
\boldsymbol{\pi})]$. The score-function identity gives
\begin{equation}
\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\mathcal{J}
=
\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{Z}\sim q_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}
\bigl[
\mathcal{L}\,
\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\log q_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{Z})
\bigr],
\label{eq:score_function}
\end{equation}
which requires only evaluations of $\mathcal{L}$, never its derivative.
With $N$ samples per iteration and a baseline $b$ tracking the running
mean of the loss, the estimator is
\begin{equation}
\widehat{\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\mathcal{J}}
=
\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}
\bigl(\mathcal{L}^{(n)}-b\bigr)
\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
\log q_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{Z}^{(n)}).
\label{eq:reinforce}
\end{equation}
The baseline leaves the estimator unbiased and reduces its variance,
which matters here because each $\mathcal{L}^{(n)}$ costs one solve of
\eqref{eq:support_problem} followed by a fixed-design evaluation, so $N$
is small.

The expectation in \eqref{eq:decision_loss} is replaced throughout
training by an average over a fixed validation set
$\mathcal{V}\subset\mathbb{P}$, held constant across iterations so that
losses from different iterations are comparable, and disjoint from the
test set on which results are reported.

% ---------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Resolution Limits}
\label{sec:resolution}

Two properties of \eqref{eq:reinforce} deserve attention because they
determine whether the procedure can work at all, independently of how it
is tuned.

First, the estimator ranks samples by $\mathcal{L}^{(n)}$, and each
$\mathcal{L}^{(n)}$ is obtained from a mixed-integer solve terminated at a
relative optimality gap $\epsilon$. Differences between samples smaller
than $\epsilon\mathcal{L}$ are therefore not ordered by the solver but by
where it happened to stop. If the spread of $\mathcal{L}^{(n)}$ within an
iteration is comparable to $\epsilon\mathcal{L}$, \eqref{eq:reinforce}
estimates the gradient of numerical noise. The gap must be chosen against
the spread of the losses being compared, not against the magnitude of the
objective, and the two can differ by orders of magnitude when the
decision moves only a small fraction of total system cost.

Second, the same threshold governs convergence. An update that changes
the loss by less than $\epsilon\mathcal{L}$ is indistinguishable from no
update, so a run whose total improvement falls below that threshold has
not been shown to have learned anything, however smooth its trajectory
appears. We report improvement against $\epsilon\mathcal{L}$ rather than
in absolute terms for this reason.

% ---------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Selection Among Candidates}
\label{sec:finalists}

A stochastic policy visits many supports, and the last iterate need not
be the best one. We retain the $F$ distinct designs with the lowest
validation loss encountered during training, together with the design
induced by the deterministic support $\boldsymbol{\mu}$, and select among
them on $\mathcal{V}$. Candidates whose losses differ by less than
$\epsilon\mathcal{L}$ are treated as tied, and ties are broken toward the
smaller installation, which avoids reporting capacity that the evaluation
cannot show to be worthwhile.

This step is a selection over sampled candidates rather than a
consequence of the gradient, and it accounts for part of the method's
advantage over selecting a support at random. Separating the two
contributions requires comparing against a search that draws the same
number of candidates from $q_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}$ with
$\boldsymbol{\theta}$ held fixed, which isolates what the policy update
adds beyond sampling and filtering.

\subsection{Evaluation Protocol}
\label{sec:protocol}

All methods are compared by the value of the design each produces, on one
test subset and against one reference. Writing
$\boldsymbol{x}^{\star}_{\mathrm{m}}$ for the design produced by method
$\mathrm{m}$ and $\mathcal{T}^{\mathrm{te}}$ for the test subset,
\begin{equation}
V_{\mathrm{m}}
=
\underbrace{
\min_{\boldsymbol{y}^{\mathrm{op}}}
\sum_{\xi\in\mathcal{T}^{\mathrm{te}}}
c(\boldsymbol{0},\xi)
}_{\text{no storage}}
-
\Bigl[
C^{\mathrm{inv}}(\boldsymbol{x}^{\star}_{\mathrm{m}})
+
\sum_{\xi\in\mathcal{T}^{\mathrm{te}}}
c(\boldsymbol{x}^{\star}_{\mathrm{m}},\xi)
\Bigr].
\label{eq:storage_value}
\end{equation}
Total system cost is a poor denominator, since the storage decision moves
a few percent of it and every design pays the same untouchable remainder;
$V_{\mathrm{m}}$ measures instead what installing storage is worth, net of
its own investment cost. Because the first term is common to all methods,
ranking by $V_{\mathrm{m}}$ and by out-of-sample cost is identical, and a
difference in $V$ is meaningful only when both designs were evaluated
against the same reference on the same subset.

Two further quantities bound the comparison. The perfect-information
value $V^{\mathrm{PI}}$, obtained by optimizing the design directly on
$\mathcal{T}^{\mathrm{te}}$, is attainable by no selection rule and
converts values into captured fractions. And because a stochastic method
must be compared against a distribution rather than a draw, we report the
spread of $V$ over repeated runs alongside its mean, and take the strongest
rule that requires neither learning nor a lucky draw as the reference
point that a learned selector must exceed.
